4. Packing Lists That Fit Your Trip

Why generated packing lists need editing, not printing

Ask any AI "what should I pack for a 10-day trip?" and you'll get 60 items that are all technically reasonable and perfectly useless as a list — because a real packing list is a decision, not a data dump. The fix is easy: constrain the AI the way a good travel writer would.

Step 1: Feed it the trip shape

Draft a packing list for this trip. Then, separately, mark each item
Essential / Nice / Cut-able, and tell me what assumptions you made.

Trip: [N days in CITY/COUNTRY, SEASON/MONTH]
Weather expectation: [e.g. 40-70°F, chance of rain, no humidity] 
  — if you're unsure of the season's weather, say so and tell me
  what to check.
Style of travel: [one carry-on only? checked bag? laundry available?]
Days with unusual needs: [hiking day X, fancy dinner day Y,
BEACH/POOL, rain-heavy city, religious sites, ...].
Traveler notes: [prescription meds, small kids, cold-sleeper, ...].

The magic phrase is "tell me what assumptions you made." Wrong assumptions (washing machines, formal dinners, snow in April) are the main failure mode of generated lists — surfacing them turns a generic list into your list.

Step 2: The three cuts that matter

Generated lists are followed by a human pass. Three questions do most of it:

  1. Cut count. "Could I buy this for less than [$10] at the destination instead of carrying it?" Umbrellas, beach towels, cheap sweaters, phone cases — yes, usually.
  2. Cut weight on the "just in case" items. Ask AI directly: "Which items on my list am I most likely to regret bringing, weighing at most 1 lb each?" — the answer is almost always the same trio: third pair of shoes, the "just in case" dress, the second book.
  3. Cut items the trip doesn't trigger. An itinerary-driven list is a from-the-start list that is proven to travel — which is why this guide fits with a print-and-binder planner kit rather than replacing it.

Step 3: The special-occasion line items

For each day with an unusual requirement (wedding guest clothes, hiking boots, a camera, a gift for a host, dressy shoes), ask:

Day 3 is a wedding. What items does THAT day alone add to my packing list,
beyond the everyday list?

You'll discover things the generic list missed: that fancy shoes also need plastic bags, that a suit needs a hanger, that a beach day needs two extra things you forgot to plan for.

Step 4: Labels that survive real life

Mark each item with a destination-based label, not a generic one:

  • carry-on — items you could not replace within 24 hours: meds, documents, one change of clothes, chargers, glasses, one comfort item. Rule: if losing the checked bag would ruin a day, that item is carry-on.
  • checked — everything replaceable.
  • wear-on-plane — heaviest items; empty water bottle; snacks.

Then run one final AI sanity check:

Here is my final list. What items am I MOST likely to have forgotten
that weigh under 100 g and would be annoying to buy within 12 hours
of landing? Top 5 only.

(The usual guilty answers: adapters, small padlock, tiny first-aid strip for blisters, a pen for landing forms, an empty cloth bag for dirty shoes.)

Copy-ready prompts

What assumptions did you make about weather and laundry availability
in my trip? List them so I can correct any that are wrong.

Cut my packing list to one carry-on. Remove anything I can buy
at the destination for a reasonable local price, and tell me
what you removed and why.
