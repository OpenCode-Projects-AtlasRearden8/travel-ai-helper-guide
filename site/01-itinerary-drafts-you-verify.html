1. Itinerary Drafts You Verify

Why "draft" is the important word

An AI-written itinerary is a draft: a fast, complete-looking sketch full of details you didn't have to invent yourself. That's exactly its value — and exactly its danger. AI tools are trained to give confident, tidy answers, and a tidy answer can be quietly wrong: a museum closed on Mondays, a train that runs "twice a day" at dawn, a neighborhood that's a 40-minute taxi ride, not a "short walk."

The workflow in this chapter makes that harmless. You get a draft in minutes, then spend ten minutes vetting only the load-bearing details — the things that would wreck a day if they were wrong.

Step 1: Give the AI your real constraints

Vague prompts get vague drafts. Before asking, decide:

  • Dates (or "first week of June" if flexible)
  • Daily start and end times — include the arrival day; it's usually a write-off
  • Travelers — kids, mobility limits, "my partner needs coffee before 9 a.m."
  • Pace — "one anchor activity per day, walk everywhere else"
  • Interest list — top 3 things you'd be sad to miss
  • Budget style — "mid-range, street food lunches, one nice dinner"

Step 2: The prompt

Copy this and fill in the brackets:

Act as a local travel planner. Draft a [N]-day itinerary for [CITY] for
[TRAVELERS], [DATES], arriving [TIME] on day 1.

Constraints:
- Pace is relaxed: max 2 major sights per day, plus wandering.
- Group: [kids / mobility needs / early risers / etc.].
- Top interests: [food markets, art, hiking, ...].
- Budget: [style]. 
- Assume we'll use public transit and walking only.

For each activity, include:
- Estimated time needed, and a realistic time to arrive.
- Known closure rules or booking requirements IF you're confident;
  write "VERIFY" instead of guessing.
- A neighborhood note: is this same-day-compatible with the other stops?

End with a list of every fact I should double-check myself, ranked by
how badly it would hurt the trip if it were wrong.

The VERIFY rule is the heart of the prompt. You're telling the AI that "verified unknown" is a better answer than "confident guess."

Step 3: Read the draft like an editor

Skim for the four failure patterns:

  1. Geographic nonsense — morning market north of the river, then a same-day museum far south of it. Ask: "regroup this day geographically."
  2. Impossible time math — "morning: hike; lunch: cooking class; afternoon: three museums" is 14 hours of optimism. Ask it to recount the hours.
  3. Closed-when-you're-there — opening days, seasons, national holidays. These all get VERIFY or a web check.
  4. Everything-is-amazing — AI drafts rarely say "skip this." If every stop got a superlative, ask: "cut two stops and tell me why."

Step 4: Verify the load-bearing details (15 minutes, no more)

Only these matter enough to check. Check each on the official site:

  • Opening hours and closed days for your top 3 sights
  • Whether any sight needs advance booking (many popular ones do)
  • First/last departure times of any transit you'll rely on
  • The one restaurant you'd be heartbroken to miss

Full checklist: see the AI verification checklist. Once those are true, small errors elsewhere won't hurt — leave salad or slack time in each day to absorb them.

Step 5: Stress-test the draft

Three follow-up prompts worth their weight:

  • "Assume it rains on day 2. Rainy-day version?"
  • "We slept badly and start at 11. Trim this day."
  • "What's the most fragile link in this plan?" — AI is surprisingly good at naming its own weak spots when asked directly.

Common worries

"Isn't booking a real agent safer?" For complex trips — multi-city rail passes, group tours, accessibility needs — yes, a human agent is worth it. AI is for the self-planned trip where it does 80% of the typing.

"The AI apologized and changed its answer — now I trust it less." Changing its answer when you push back is normal and fine. Don't resolve disagreements by arguing; resolve them by verifying on the primary source. That's the whole system.

Reality check to keep in mind: an itinerary draft usually needs one round of human correction — usually the geographic regroup or the hours recount. Plan for that round and it stops being a surprise.

Copy-ready prompts

Regroup day 2 geographically so we walk in one direction all day.

Recount the hours in each day. Flag anything with more scheduled
time than actually exists between [start] and [dinner].

What are the three most fragile facts in this itinerary,
ranked by damage if wrong?
