# 6. Scam-Resistant Booking Habits

## Why this chapter exists

AI makes scammers *more* productive too. Fake listings get cleaner photos and
fluenter messages; phishing sites look more real; and travelers rushing to catch
a deal are exactly the target. Nothing here requires paranoia — just six habits,
each of which takes seconds once learned. (AI doesn't need to be involved in
your *booking* at all; it's mainly in your *research*. The scams here mostly
appear at the moment you switch from researching to paying.)

## The six habits

### Habit 1: Verify the seller's domain, not the vibe

Before paying on any travel site, look at the address bar. Official sellers:

- **Airline tickets:** the airline's own domain (book direct from the airline
  when the price is comparable — see [chapter 3](03-hotel-and-flight-research-honesty.md)).
- **Hotels:** the hotel domain, or a major booking platform you have actually
  heard of, or a metasearch site *redirecting you to* one of those.
- **Rail:** the operator's own site (e.g. the national rail operator), not a
  "discount" reseller with a bunch of hyphens in the name.

Red flags in domains: lots of hyphens, extra words ("book", "discount", "cheap"
in the domain), a .net or .info where you expect .com, or a domain that looks
like a famous brand with one letter changed. If AI *found* a website for you,
treat that exactly as a reason to double-check its domain — AI has been known
to invent plausible-but-wrong URLs.

### Habit 2: Pay in the safe channel

Prefer paying in a channel with genuine buyer protection: a credit card, or a
platform's own payment system. A few locks worth keeping:

- **Avoid wire transfers, gift-card payments, "friend" payments, or crypto** for
  anything travel-related. Legitimate bookings almost never need these.
- **Never send a payment, deposit, or "tax fee" because a chat message told you
  to.** If a chat assistant ever shows you a payment link or asks you to do a
  transaction inside a conversation, that's a red flag: the real versions of
  these tools do not take payments for you.
- **Price sanity check:** if a price is dramatically below every other source
  (a $500 week in Paris, a $89 transatlantic fare), assume scam or bait until
  proven otherwise. AI can be used here for a sanity check — ask it "is this
  price realistic for [route/season]? Don't sugar-coat it."

### Habit 3: Treat "last chance" pressure as a scam signal

Scarcity and countdown pressure are the scammer's best friends. Real inventory
exists, but "only 2 left!" and "prices will expire in 10 minutes" on a
*text-message* or *chat* channel is a distress signal. Close it, sleep, and
recheck in the morning. Good deals for your dates are generally still there in
the morning, and scams rely on you *not* sleeping on it.

### Habit 4: Confirm directly, then only once

After booking: confirm with the *provider itself*, directly, once. For a hotel,
a one-line email to the hotel (using only the address printed on their official
site) is cheap insurance. For flights, check the airline's "manage my booking"
page with the reference number from the airline (not a travel agent's). Skip
anything that says "do not confirm directly with us or with them."

### Habit 5: Keep a paper trail you can reach offline

- Screenshot the booking confirmation **and** the cancellation policy.
- Save PDF/Voucher versions offline in your notes app (chapter 5's offline kit).
  Airport check-in desks and train station counters are notorious dead zones.
- One line on a printed page (see the
  [trip worksheet](trip-worksheet.md)) — the
  reference numbers you actually need — has rescued many trips.

### Habit 6: Use AI as your scam auditor, not your booking agent

AI is genuinely good at scam-spotting when it's on your side:

```
I got this message after booking: [PASTE MESSAGE].
Here is the website address it came from: [PASTE URL].
Is this consistent with how legitimate travel platforms and hotels
communicate? What parts would a scammer be likely to use? Be specific.
```

```
Analyze this cancellation policy: [PASTE].
Explain to me like I'm new: what exact actions by me will or won't
get a refund, and by when?
```

Rules of the road for this use: paste **only the message itself** (with names
and reference numbers whited-out), never the confirmation codes, never your
own contact info, never full card last-digits. AI doesn't need your location,
your booking references, or your passport to assess a scam. Scammers in a fake
"customer support chat" will be more than happy to collect any of it.

## The 60-second pre-payment drill

Before entering card details anywhere, run this:

1. Domain check (habit 1). SSL padlock isn't enough — any scammer can buy one.
2. How did I get here? Search engine/brand memory = fine. Chat link/unexpected
   email link = extra suspicion.
3. Price sanity check against a real search or second source.
4. Payment method is protected (habit 2).
5. After payment: save confirmation offline (habit 5) and later confirm once
   with the provider (habit 4).

That whole drill costs less than a minute and defeats a large share of
travel-booking scams.

## What to do if you already paid a scammer

- **Speed matters more than pride.** Call your card issuer immediately and
  request a chargeback — hours matter here, not days.
- Change any password you re-used on that site.
- Report to the real platform if the fake impersonated one (booking sites have
  fraud-report forms; airlines too).
- Beware the *second* scam — "we can recover your money for a small fee" is
  scammers selling to scam victims. Only your bank can recover your money.
